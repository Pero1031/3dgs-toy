# The gloss ceiling is broken by "sharing" and "tracing"

3D Gaussian Splatting and its predecessor NeRF have handled gloss by moving view-dependent information out of the points. The progression goes from "each point memorises a view-dependent colour", to "shared lighting looked up by the reflection direction", and then to "tracing that casts reflection rays into the scene itself". **The line of work that replaces SH with spherical Gaussians, spherical betas, spherical Voronoi, ASG and so on gains only 0.3–1 dB on standard data, and the authors themselves write that mirror-like reflections are not fixed.** This is the same picture as this repo's failure mode (1): with a median of 26 observations per point, raising the SH degree only memorises the training views, and the test views stop at 24–27 dB. The shared-environment line that inherits the Ref-NeRF pattern (GaussianShader → 3DGS-DR → Ref-GS / Ref-Gaussian) raises Shiny Blender from 30.4 dB to 34–35 dB. On the real-capture Ref-Real, however, it is roughly level with plain 3DGS (23.85 dB). So the literature also reproduces failure mode (2), where the light positions come out correctly but, over the statue as a whole, the result loses to per-point SH. Only the ray-tracing line (NeRF-Casting, NDE, EnvGS, IRGS, Poirier-Ginter et al.) addresses the statue's reflections of itself (3) head-on, and only the last of these has explicit multiple bounces. The contribution of interreflection measured on existing benchmarks is 0.2–0.6 dB, which is far from a statue where 38% of the reflection rays hit the statue itself. **No glossy 3DGS method was found that converts sub-pixel bumps (4) into roughness as the normal distribution within the pixel footprint; this is the most direct gap.** On the M4000 (sm_52, Windows 10, 8 GB), every official Gaussian-based codebase requires a source build of a CUDA extension, and the more a line of work helps with (3), the more it assumes OptiX and RT cores. A simplified port into 3dgs-toy is therefore the only realistic path. As a first move, the most instructive configuration is deferred shading + a shared cubemap + a per-point low-degree SH residual, with a switch on top driven by the visibility of the reflection ray computed from the true mesh, because it lets failure modes (1)–(3) be separated one at a time.

## Replacing SH with another spherical function does not move the near-mirror ceiling

SH replacements fall into two types. The first type uses an independent analytic function per point: SG-Splatting, which mixes spherical Gaussians with low-degree SH; the 2-lobe, 15-parameter spherical beta (Deformable Beta Splatting); the 48-parameter spherical Voronoi with a learnable partition of directions (SV, CVPR 2026); and the 12–39-parameter NASGabor, which adds a cosine carrier to an anisotropic Gaussian (EGSR 2026). 6DGS (ICLR 2025), which slices a 6D Gaussian over position and direction by the view ray, the N-dimensional beta of UBS, and VoD-3DGS, which makes opacity view-dependent, belong to the same type ([SB](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.18630), [SV](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.14180), [NASGabor](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.09794), [6DGS](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.04974)). The second type converts per-point features into colour with a shared network. The representative, Spec-Gaussian (NeurIPS 2024), is often described as "replacing SH with ASG". In fact it predicts the parameters of 32 ASGs placed at fixed positions on the hemisphere from a 24-dimensional per-point feature with a shared MLP, and then outputs the specular colour with a shared 3-layer, 64-unit MLP, so it belongs to this type ([Spec-Gaussian](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.15870), [NeurIPS 2024](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/hash/708e0d691a22212e1e373dc8779cbe53-Abstract.html)). The anchor features of Scaffold-GS and the 816-parameter shared MLP of Hahlbohm et al. are of the same type ([Scaffold-GS](https://city-super.github.io/scaffold-gs/), [Hahlbohm et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.05255)). The per-point designs all aim for "sharper lobes" within **a budget of 12–48 parameters, about the same as degree-3 SH**. Whether a near-mirror appearance can be interpolated between training views is not their subject.

The gains are small. In the SV comparison, which swaps only the view-dependent colour, the gain from SH to SV is only 34.15 → 34.58 dB on NeRF-Synthetic and 28.09 → 28.71 dB on Mip-NeRF360 ([SV](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.14180)). The third-party study by Hahlbohm et al. writes that, averaged over 5 runs under the same conditions, most of the differences between SH, SV, NASG, NASGabor and neural variants fall within the run-to-run variation ([Hahlbohm et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.05255)). Only anisotropic highlights improve substantially. Spec-Gaussian reached 33.82 → 37.70 dB on its own Anisotropic Synthetic, but reports no numbers for Shiny Blender, Glossy Synthetic or Ref-Real ([Spec-Gaussian](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.15870)). The 36.09 dB that SV reaches on Shiny Blender is the value for its reflection mode, which uses a shared set of light probes and a shared cubemap; each point holds only a diffuse colour and a roughness. In the ablation that changes the probe contents from SG to SV, the result rises from 34.19 → 36.09 dB, so **directional expressiveness only pays off when combined with a shared lighting representation** ([SV](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.14180)). The self-reported limitations also agree. DBS writes that it is weak at mirror-like reflections and anisotropic highlights. Hahlbohm et al. state that no spherical colour function can represent specular reflection accurately unless secondary rays are traced explicitly. Spec-Gaussian admits that reflections depend on the environment and the geometry and so cannot be separated from the material ([SB](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.18630), [Hahlbohm et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.05255), [Spec-Gaussian](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.15870)).

On generalisation, no 3DGS-family paper was found that gives numbers for the gap between training and test views on glossy data. Two comparative studies do say the same thing qualitatively. Miazga et al. write that on outdoor scenes with sparse views, highly expressive functions overfit and SH interpolates more smoothly ([NASGabor](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.09794)). Hahlbohm et al. summarise that a shared MLP fits the training images far better than explicit spherical functions, and that SH is the least prone to overfitting ([Hahlbohm et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.05255)). This repo's point-by-point fit is a quantitative version of this tendency. The best test-view result for gold is 24.0 dB at L=2–3. It starts to fall from L ≥ 4, where the number of coefficients (L+1)² exceeds the median observation count of 26, and at L=16 the gap widens to 56.1 dB on training and 13.8 dB on test. Even with a smoothness penalty it levels off at 26.3–26.8 dB. Gold in 3DGS proper reaches only 22.8 dB even on the training views, which does not even reach the point-by-point fit at L=3 (28.6 dB) ([this repo's experiment](../README.md)). What a shared decoder shares is "the shape of the function of direction", not "what is reflected", so the gaps between observations are not filled. A scheme like 6DGS, which puts the directional dependence into the shift of the centre and into the opacity, adds the freedom to assign a different Gaussian to each training view, so with sparse observations it should push towards overfitting, but there is no data that tests this. This line of work is therefore not a solution for the gold statue. It is reasonable to use it as a cheap control to confirm on 3dgs-toy that the ceiling is set by the coarseness of the observations, and it can be tried just by swapping the call to `eval_sh` in `gs/render.py`.

## Sharing lighting by reflection direction gains 4 dB on synthetic benchmarks, but normals decide the outcome

The origin, Ref-NeRF (CVPR 2022), set up four pillars: feed the directional MLP the reflection direction ω_r = 2(ω_o·n)n − ω_o instead of the view direction; split the colour into diffuse and specular as γ(c_d + s ⊙ c_s); use IDE, which attenuates the higher degrees of the SH encoding by A_ℓ ≈ exp(−ℓ(ℓ+1)ρ/2) according to the roughness ρ; and use predicted normals in place of the noisy density-gradient normals, constrained by a predicted-normal loss and a penalty on back-facing normals ([Ref-NeRF](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2112.03907)). In the ablations, **the handling of normals dominates performance, and the lighting encoding has almost no effect**. Against the full model's 35.96 dB and 18.38° normal error on Shiny Blender, removing predicted normals drops to 30.91 dB and 30.67°, removing the orientation penalty drops to 31.62 dB and 52.56°, and removing the reflection direction drops to 29.47 dB, but replacing IDE with positional encoding gives 35.90 dB, almost unchanged ([Ref-NeRF](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2112.03907)). The gain on real captures is small: 25.53 → 25.65 dB on sedan, and on garden spheres, with its row of spheres that reflect the surroundings, SSIM falls from 0.620 → 0.601. The authors list interreflection and non-distant lighting as limitations ([Ref-NeRF](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2112.03907)).

The 3DGS family inherits this structure almost unchanged and has improved how normals are obtained and how lighting is stored.

| Method (venue) | Shared lighting | Roughness | How normals are obtained | Fallback for what shared lighting cannot explain | Shiny Synthetic PSNR / normal MAE | Ref-Real PSNR |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

| 3DGS-DR (SIGGRAPH 2024) | Environment map (single-level bilinear lookup) | No | Shortest axis composited to pixels by deferred shading, normal propagation | SH base colour | 33.94 / 2.43° | 23.80 |
| Ref-GS (CVPR 2025) | Spherical Mip grid 512×1024×16, 9 levels | Yes | 2DGS surfels + depth distortion and depth-normal consistency | Outer product of spatial and directional features | 34.80 / 2.21° | 23.93 |
| Ref-Gaussian (ICLR 2025) | split-sum (LUT + pre-integrated cubemap) | Yes | Cross product of 2DGS tangent vectors, material-aware normal propagation | Per-point SH indirect light for directions occluded by the mesh | 34.92 / 2.17° | 23.95 |
| GS-ROR2 (TOG 2025) | Environment map + split-sum | Yes | Shortest axis + residual, mutually supervised with TensoSDF | None (direct light only) | 32.16 / 2.64° | — |
| Ref-DGS (SIGGRAPH 2026) | Spherical feature map | Yes | Only the geometry 2DGS outputs normals | A separate set of Gaussians for local reflections | 35.21 / 1.43° | 24.89 |

All numbers are taken from the unified comparison table of Ref-DGS. Plain 3DGS does not appear in that table ([Ref-DGS](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.07664)).

The substance of every one of these refinements is an effort to get the normals right. GaussianShader exploits the fact that Gaussians flatten during optimisation and takes the shortest axis as the normal, correcting it with a residual and with consistency against depth-derived normals. Removing the consistency loss drops it from 32.09 → 30.93 dB ([GaussianShader](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.17977)). 3DGS-DR pointed out that in forward shading, where lighting is looked up per Gaussian and then composited, the gradients of the Gaussians are independent, whereas in deferred shading, where the normals are composited to the pixel and lighting is looked up once, overlapping Gaussians are tied together through the gradient. This raised the PSNR of ball from 27.64 dB (forward) to 33.66 dB (deferred). It also showed that removing "normal propagation", which periodically enlarges the faces of reflective Gaussians to pass normals to their neighbours, brings it back to 27.85 dB ([3DGS-DR](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.18454)). In Ref-Gaussian, removing 2DGS doubles the normal error from 2.15° → 4.45°, so the flat primitive itself supports the quality of the normals ([Ref-Gaussian](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.19282)). The family as a whole has converged on a two-layer structure: distant lighting shared by all primitives, plus a per-point or per-pixel correction term that takes whatever the shared lighting cannot explain.

The fact that this two-layer structure does not work on real captures matches this repo's failure mode (2). Comparing within the same table: in the Ref-GS table, plain 3DGS is at 23.85 dB against GaussianShader at 23.46 dB and 3DGS-DR at 23.80 dB ([Ref-GS](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.00905)), and in the EnvGS table too, 3DGS at 23.70 dB beats 3DGS-DR at 23.52 dB and GaussianShader at 22.88 dB ([EnvGS](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.15215)). Only Ref-DGS (24.89 dB), which has an explicit fallback for nearby reflections, and the ray-tracing EnvGS (24.55 dB) are clearly ahead ([Ref-DGS](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.07664)). In this repo's shared-lighting fit, the 4 sphere lights appeared at their true positions from the gold reflections alone. Yet over all observations, per-point constant + shared lighting gave 22.0 dB and per-point degree-2 SH + shared lighting gave 24.5 dB, short of the 25.9 dB of per-point SH. Only when restricted to unoccluded observations with few bumps did the order reverse, at 28.8 dB versus 27.9 dB ([this repo's experiment](../README.md)). "Per-point degree-2 SH + shared lighting" is exactly the two-layer structure of the literature, so adding a fallback alone is not enough. Also, the large effect of normals on the benchmarks is the flip side of the estimated normals being poor. In this repo's experiment with the true geometry, replacing face normals with vertex-interpolated normals changed the result by only 0.2–0.4 dB, which can be read as the bottleneck having already moved from normals to (3) and (4).

Near-mirror gold adds two problems of its own. One is the resolution of the lighting representation. GS-ROR2 states explicitly that split-sum blurs at extremely low roughness ([GS-ROR2](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.18544)). The 64-pixel-per-face cubemap of GaussianShader is about 1.4° per texel (90°/64, author's calculation), and the degree-32 SH environment this repo used is about 6° ([this repo's experiment](../README.md)). Both are coarser than the lobe width of GGX α 0.01 (around 1°). The other is the violation of the distant-lighting assumption. The edge of the statue is up to about 0.25 m from the centre and the lights are about 2 m away, so the offset between the reflection direction and the light direction is up to atan(0.25/2) ≈ 7° (author's calculation), several times the lobe width. 3DGS-DR has an option for real captures that confines the environment light to within a sphere around the object ([3DGS-DR](https://github.com/gapszju/3DGS-DR)), but this is an engineering workaround that shuts nearby objects out of the lighting; it does not represent lights at a finite distance. The inverse-rendering line that separates lighting from material (GS-IR, R3DG) and translucency were covered in an existing survey (outside the repository, in Japanese: `D:\GoLD_rayt\out\studies\nerf_survey\reports\NeRF質感再構成の文献調査.md`). In relighting of glossy objects, the GS-ROR2 table has GS-IR at 16.26 dB and R3DG at 17.09 dB, far behind the 26.63 dB of NeRO, which has an SDF, so there is little reason to bring them into novel view synthesis of the gold statue ([GS-ROR2](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.18544)).

## The 38% of reflection rays that hit the statue itself can only be handled by methods that cast rays into the scene

The origins of near-field light and interreflection are on the NeRF side. NeRF-Casting (TOG) uses the Zip-NeRF hash grid as its backbone, computes the reflection direction from the termination point and normal composited along the ray, and approximates with 5 reflection rays a vMF cone whose width is "the pixel radius + the composited roughness". Each ray samples 64 points and integrates features rather than colour, which a small MLP converts to colour. Reflections of nearby objects, such as the head of a statue being reflected, are its central claim. It explicitly traces only one reflection, and training takes about 100 minutes on 8 V100s ([NeRF-Casting](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.14871)). NDE (CVPR 2024) represents the near-field component by roughness-dependent cone tracing of tri-plane features (radius √3·ρ²·distance) and the far-field component by a feature cubemap prefiltered with GGX. It reaches 34.08 dB on the teaser scene that shows interreflection (Ref-NeRF: 28.18 dB), but admits that multi-bounce reflections remain even when the object is removed; the second and later bounces are baked into the features. Training takes 10–18 hours on an RTX 3090, and it is sensitive to normal quality and breaks down with a hash-grid SDF ([NDE](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.14847)). UniSDF (NeurIPS 2024) blends a camera-view and a reflected-view radiance field and does not trace near-field light explicitly ([UniSDF](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/hash/05b12f103c9e613efc4c85674cdc9066-Abstract.html)).

The 3DGS family handles nearby reflections in five ways. EnvGS (CVPR 2025) traces reflection rays with OptiX into "environment Gaussians" that are separate from the surface ones, and does not assume distant lighting ([EnvGS](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.15215)). Ref-Gaussian extracts a mesh with TSDF every 3000 steps and uses a BVH only to decide the visibility of the reflection ray; in occluded directions it uses indirect light obtained by evaluating each Gaussian's SH in the reflection direction ([Ref-Gaussian](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.19282)). IRGS (CVPR 2025) traces into the same 2D Gaussians and is a radiance-cache scheme that returns, at the hit point, the view-dependent colour learned in the first stage. It uses 256 samples per pixel, so rendering one image takes about 11 seconds ([IRGS](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.15867)). Poirier-Ginter et al. (SIGGRAPH Asia 2025) supervise with diffuse and specular layers and with normal, roughness and F0 buffers, and perform path tracing on the Gaussians with up to 3 bounces during training. As far as was checked, it is the only method that handles multiple bounces explicitly ([Poirier-Ginter et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.30861v1)). Ref-DGS represents self-reflection and interreflection by splatting local-reflection Gaussians without ray tracing, and criticises the ray-tracing line for making training much slower ([Ref-DGS](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.07664)). SpecTRe-GS (CVPR 2025), which traces only highly specular surfaces and renders rough surfaces by splatting, is a design that suits the α 0.01 statue, but its text and numbers could not be obtained, so it could not be evaluated ([SpecTRe-GS](https://spectre-gs.github.io)).

The weight of interreflection on the benchmarks is small. Ref-Gaussian drops only from 30.33 → 30.14 dB without interreflection, Ref-GS only from 34.00 → 33.37 dB without the spatial features, and NeRF-Casting only from 33.91 → 33.30 dB without nearby reflections. The authors of Ref-Gaussian state that interreflection is a minority effect on Glossy Synthetic ([Ref-Gaussian](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.19282), [Ref-GS](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.00905), [NeRF-Casting](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.14871)). The advantage of ray tracing shows on scene-level real captures where nearby objects are reflected. EnvGS reaches 24.62 dB on Ref-Real (3DGS-DR: 23.52 dB) and 46.39 dB in PSNR over the near-field regions only (runner-up: 44.16 dB), but on the object-level Shiny Blender its 6-scene average of 33.83 dB is below the 33.96 dB of 3DGS-DR ([EnvGS](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.15215)). A physically correct decomposition is hard to reconcile with the PSNR of the final image. On Shiny Kitchen, the diffuse layer / specular layer / final image are 33.20 / 24.30 / 26.96 dB for Poirier-Ginter et al. and 14.33 / 14.48 / 32.82 dB for EnvGS. In fidelity of the final image, EnvGS, which can place "fake geometry" behind the mirror, comes out ahead ([Poirier-Ginter et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.30861v1)).

Applied to the gold statue, each line reaches a different point. The structure of Ref-Gaussian is almost the same form as this repo's "shared lighting + per-point SH" with an explicit visibility switch added. It can prevent the top quarter of the occluded observations (luminance 0.55 or higher) from contaminating the environment map, but the reflections on the occluded side are in the end stored in per-point SH, so the ceiling of failure mode (1) remains there. With an IRGS-style cache, the outgoing radiance at the hit point is itself a sharp gold reflection, so a low-order cache cannot fully represent the brightness of "lit gold reflected in gold". Only Poirier-Ginter et al., who recurse explicitly, can handle the gold → gold → light path physically, and their main evaluation setting (synthetic scenes with ground-truth buffers) matches the position of this repo's author, who can output normals and specular layers with GoLD_rayt. The finite distance of the lights can be represented as parallax by EnvGS or NeRF-Casting, which hold the lights as 3D elements. However, the environment Gaussians of EnvGS are initialised on a grid over the extent of the SfM point cloud (the 99.5% quantile bounding box), so if the lights about 2 m away are outside the captured extent, they may not be placed unless the extent is enlarged (conjecture) ([EnvGS](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.15215)).

### Planar-mirror methods rely on the closed form of mirror reflection, so they do not transfer to a curved statue

Restricted to planar mirrors, secondary rays can be written in closed form as a mirror reflection. MirrorGaussian (ECCV 2024) fits a plane with RANSAC from the mirror outline obtained with SAM and from the SfM points, renders mirrored copies of the Gaussians with the same rasterizer, composites them with the mirror mask, and keeps about 155 FPS on a V100 ([MirrorGaussian](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.11921)). Mirror-3DGS renders from the mirrored camera and, on real captures, reaches 25.93 dB against 22.55 dB for 3DGS, and 24.53 dB on the mirror region alone ([Mirror-3DGS](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.01168v2)). Both assume a mirror mask and a single plane. Curved surfaces, rough reflection, self-reflection and facing mirrors are out of scope, so they cannot be applied to the gold statue. Still, the idea of "not placing fake objects behind the mirror, and explaining the reflecting surface and the reflected object with the same representation" is a prototype that leads towards EnvGS and Poirier-Ginter et al.

## There is not yet a glossy 3DGS that converts sub-pixel bumps into effective roughness

The main methods all set the blur width by a per-point (per-Gaussian) roughness ρ. There are three mechanisms: IDE, which attenuates the higher degrees of the directional encoding; split-sum, the spherical Mip grid and NDE, which look up a prefiltered mip level according to the roughness; and NeRF-Casting, which widens the reflection cone. Without roughness, Ref-NeRF drops from 35.96 → 33.39 dB, and Ref-GS drops from 34.00 → 30.12 dB without the mip ([Ref-NeRF](https://arxiv.org/html/2112.03907v1), [Ref-GS](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.00905)). 3DGS-DR, EnvGS and Spec-Gaussian have no roughness and let blurred reflections escape into the base colour or the blend weights ([3DGS-DR](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.18454v2), [EnvGS](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.15215)). The only one that puts the pixel size into the reflection blur is NeRF-Casting, with its cone width κ̄⁻¹ = ṙ + ρ̄, but ṙ is the angular radius of the pixel, not the normal variation within the pixel ([NeRF-Casting](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.14871)). The one that addressed the insufficient resolution of normals head-on is TextureSplat. Arguing that one normal per primitive makes it hard to capture high-frequency normal changes, it gave each 2D Gaussian a 2×2 normal and material texture. On Shiny Blender it improved from Ref-Gaussian 34.89 dB → with normal mapping added 35.33 dB → full model 35.90 dB, and the normal error went from 2.078° → 1.78°. But because 2DGS is not anti-aliased, mipmaps cannot be used, and minification artifacts remain ([TextureSplat](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.13348)). Neural Microfacet Fields, which treats each volume sample as a microfacet, also does not argue that aggregation over the volume becomes an effective roughness, and it writes that, assuming distant lighting, its performance drops on coffee, which has nearby lights ([NMF](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2303.17806)). On the graphics side, the method of Kaplanyan et al. (HPG 2016), which maps the pixel footprint into slope space and filters the normal distribution, handles "effective roughness" explicitly ([Kaplanyan et al.](https://research.nvidia.com/sites/default/files/pubs/2016-06_Filtering-Distributions-of/NDFFiltering.pdf)), but no example of bringing this into glossy reconstruction with NeRF or 3DGS was found in 3 searches.

The numbers for this repo's statue show the size of this gap. Within the extent of 1 pixel, the normals vary by a median of 8.2° and a 90th percentile of 27.6° ([this repo's experiment](../README.md)). By the survey's rough conversion, treating the angular variation as the standard deviation of the slope, this corresponds to roughly 0.2–0.75 in the α of a Beckmann distribution, 20–75 times the material's α = 0.01. The factor changes with the definition of the variation, but the conclusion that it differs by more than an order of magnitude does not. (Addendum 2026-11-18: this variation is the angle obtained as acos|n̄| from the length of the mean normal, so α is about the same as the variation angle in radians, and 0.14–0.48 is correct. Also, it is the value measured with face normals, and it becomes smaller with the interpolated normals that the renderer uses. For details see [Follow-up 3 of this repo's experiment](../README.md).) What a per-point ρ can absorb is only a symmetric, single-peaked lobe centred on the mean reflection direction. When the normals within a pixel split into two peaks across the ridge of a fold in the drapery, raising ρ does not make the colour match, and the error is pushed into the shared environment (conjecture). The order of shading is also involved. Deferred shading looks up the lighting only once with the mean normal composited at the pixel, so the normal distribution within the pixel is collapsed. Forward shading averages the radiance within the pixel, so physically it is closer to an integral over the footprint. Yet 3DGS-DR reports that forward is 6 dB worse, for reasons of the gradient ([3DGS-DR](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.18454)). No paper was found that discusses this trade-off between "how easily the gradient flows" and "reproducing the normal distribution within the pixel".

### Existing benchmarks are biased towards smooth objects under distant lighting, and the gold statue lies outside them

The standard evaluation data is concentrated on setups that orbit a central object. Shiny Blender has 6 objects (ball, car, coffee, helmet, teapot, toaster), 100 training and 200 test views, and is lit mainly by a distant environment map ([Ref-NeRF](https://arxiv.org/html/2112.03907v1), [SpecNeRF](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.13102v3)). Glossy Synthetic has 8 objects rendered with Cycles, 128 images at 800×800, lit by indoor HDRs from PolyHaven, and includes the statue-like angel, horse and luyu ([NeRO](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.17398)). However, the Glossy in the 3DGS-DR table is 6 objects, excluding angel and horse, and the Glossy values of Ref-GS agree with this, so statue-type objects are left out of the widely used average ([3DGS-DR](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.18454v2), [Ref-GS](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.00905)). Ref-Real (3 real scenes) and the 4 real scenes of NeRF-Casting (230–350 images) contain nearby reflections but have no ground-truth geometry ([NeRF-Casting](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.14871)). The difference in difficulty between objects is large: Ref-NeRF reaches 47.46 dB and a normal error of 1.55° on ball, but only 25.70 dB and 42.87° on toaster, which has many concavities ([Ref-NeRF](https://arxiv.org/html/2112.03907v1)). The numbers vary between papers: Ref-NeRF on Shiny Blender ranges over 32.32–35.96 dB, and the normal error of GaussianShader over 7.00–22.31°, depending on the table, so comparisons must be kept within the same table ([Ref-GS](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.00905), [3DGS-DR](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.18454)).

This repo's data lies outside this distribution on all four axes. At 256 px, the area of 1 pixel is about 9.8 times that at 800 px; 90 training views is fewer than the existing data (100–350 images); gold at α 0.01 is near-mirror; and the lights are close, at 2 m. On the other hand, having ground truth for normals, visibility and material all together is a strength that the existing synthetic data does not have either. The Blender sources of Shiny Blender are public ([Ref-NeRF project](https://dorverbin.github.io/refnerf/)), so measuring the same "normal variation within 1 pixel" on the existing objects would show in numbers that the existing benchmarks contain little fine geometry.

## No official code runs on the M4000, and the more effective a line is, the more it needs OptiX and RT cores

The constraints of the old GPU come in three layers. First, the official Gaussian-based codebases all assume a pip source build of a CUDA extension that is a modified 3DGS / 2DGS rasterizer, and the official 3DGS itself lists as requirements Compute Capability 7.0 or higher and 24 GB of VRAM for paper-quality training ([3DGS](https://github.com/graphdeco-inria/gaussian-splatting)). Under the 3dgs-toy constraint of "no building of CUDA extensions", none of them can be run. Second, the ray-tracing methods EnvGS, IRGS and 3DGRT need OptiX. OptiX 7.x supports Compute Capability 5.0 or higher ([NVIDIA forum](https://forums.developer.nvidia.com/t/optix-7-1-issue-with-running-samples-on-a-maxwell-card/140118/2)), but OptiX 9.1 is limited to Turing or later and the R590 driver ([OptiX](https://developer.nvidia.com/designworks/optix/download)), and the last driver that supports Maxwell is the R580 series (581.80 on Windows) ([Tom's Hardware](https://www.tomshardware.com/pc-components/gpu-drivers/nvidia-to-axe-maxwell-pascal-and-volta-gpus-with-end-of-driver-support-580-series-drivers-will-be-the-last-to-support-gtx-900-and-1000-cards)). Without RT cores, BVH traversal is done in software, and 3dgrut itself writes that it is slower than 3DGS even with RT cores, so running training on the M4000 is judged unrealistic ([3dgrut](https://github.com/nv-tlabs/3dgrut)). Third, PyTorch drops Maxwell from builds with CUDA 12.8 and later, and PyTorch 2.14 is the last with CUDA 12.6 wheels ([PyTorch 12.8 announcement](https://dev-discuss.pytorch.org/t/cuda-toolkit-version-and-architecture-support-update-maxwell-and-pascal-architecture-support-removed-in-cuda-12-8-and-12-9-builds/3128), [PyTorch 12.6 announcement](https://dev-discuss.pytorch.org/t/notice-cuda-12-6-wheels-will-no-longer-be-published-from-pytorch-2-15-drops-maxwell-pascal-volta/3432)). This repo's PyTorch 2.7.1+cu118 is usable for now, but it will eventually be unable to follow dependencies that assume newer versions (nvdiffrast requires PyTorch 2.7 or higher). For none of the methods below was a report found of them working or failing on Maxwell, so every outlook in the table is an estimate from the dependencies.

## For the gold statue, the effective order is a deferred-shading shared environment with a visibility switch layered on top

Setting the literature against this repo's diagnosis, the ranking of the lines likely to help is clear. The most effective is the shared-environment line. In this repo's fit, unoccluded observations make up about 62% of the total, and on unoccluded observations with few bumps, shared lighting beat per-point SH by 0.8 dB ([this repo's experiment](../README.md)). But that holds only when the occluded observations are shut out of the lighting, so the second most effective is a Ref-Gaussian-style visibility switch. The switch only turns the occluded 38% from "outliers that contaminate the lighting" into "observations that the per-point residual takes care of", and the ceiling for that part stays at the same 24–27 dB as failure mode (1) (conjecture). What really breaks this ceiling is tracing into the scene, and the bright gold → gold reflections in particular need explicit recursion, but on the M4000 this is limited to a check at reduced scale. For (4), the approach is to absorb the symmetric part with per-point roughness and attack the rest with a device not in the literature: "building an effective roughness from the normal distribution within the pixel". SH replacement and planar-mirror methods are not solutions for this statue.

### The first step is deferred shading, which removes the fixed 3 channels

The changes to 3dgs-toy are small. The current `composite` writes the composited result into `torch.zeros(P, n_tiles, 3)`, so the channel count is fixed at 3, but `to_image`, which rearranges it into an image, does not depend on the channel count. So a G-buffer can be output just by replacing the 3 with `color.shape[-1]` and giving the background per channel ([rasterize.py](../../../gs/rasterize.py)). The attributes to composite come to about 9 channels: 3 for the low-degree SH base colour, 3 for the normal (the shortest axis flipped towards the camera), 1 for reflection strength, 1 for roughness and 1 for depth. The (pixels × intersections) intermediate tensor grows roughly in proportion to the channel count, but this can be absorbed by the existing chunking via `max_entries` and by gradient checkpointing. Per pixel, the minimal 3DGS-DR-style configuration is to compute the reflection direction from the composited normal, look up a cubemap [6,3,L,L], with its 6 faces laid out in one image, using `grid_sample`, and combine as (1−R)·C + R·E(r) ([3DGS-DR](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.18454)). Because the edges of the lights are sharp, the lighting is held in texels rather than SH, at around 128 pixels per face (about 0.7° per texel, author's calculation). The levels for different roughness are made with `avg_pool2d`, and interpolating between 2 adjacent levels gives a simplified version of split-sum. Making it deferred is the key point: with forward shading the gradients of the Gaussians are independent, and the normals do not line up with their neighbours.

### The second step is to remove the oracles one at a time and separate the failure modes

Start with a "normal oracle", which fixes the normals with a normal map rendered from the true mesh and learns only the lighting. Next, replace it with shortest-axis normals constrained by a flattening penalty and a consistency loss against normals derived from the composited depth, and measure the difference from the oracle. The finite distance of the lights can be approximated by a parallax correction that intersects the reflection ray with a proxy sphere of radius about 2 m around the centre of the statue and looks up the cubemap in the direction of the intersection point (author's proposal). An offset of up to 7° is fatal for a 1° lobe, so even on the convex-shape control, a difference should appear with and without this correction. Then add visibility. This repo already has ray marching on a 2 mm grid in `env_diagnose.py`, so it is enough to precompute, under `torch.no_grad`, an occlusion mask for the reflection rays for each training view and, at occluded pixels, composite the value of each Gaussian's SH evaluated in the reflection direction in place of the environment map. This corresponds to a simplified version of Eq. 10 of Ref-Gaussian ([Ref-Gaussian](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.19282)). The mesh is fixed, so the occlusion needs to be computed only once per view, and there is no cost during training. If the pixels at the test views that fall under failure mode (3) are tallied separately, it shows how much the visibility switch reduces the contamination of the lighting and where the occluded side levels off.

### The third step is an experiment not in the literature: adding the normal distribution within the pixel to the roughness

For the composited normal, the further its length |n| before normalisation falls below 1, the more the normals within the pixel vary. So add a correction that adds the variation estimated from |n| to the roughness (the cubemap level). This corresponds to a simplification of normal distribution filtering in graphics ([Kaplanyan et al.](https://research.nvidia.com/sites/default/files/pubs/2016-06_Filtering-Distributions-of/NDFFiltering.pdf)). Alongside it, run an oracle that supplies the normal variation within the pixel, measured from the true mesh, as a roughness map. If the 28° tail can be explained by an "effective roughness", the oracle should improve things greatly; if pixels that split into two peaks remain, the improvement should level off. Either result would be a finding not in the existing research. Conversely, there are things not recommended. Screen-space reflection is light, because it advances the reflection ray over the already composited depth and colour, but the concavities deep in the folds of the statue that reflect the statue itself are often occluded from a frontal view and cannot be captured. A scheme that evaluates all Gaussians by brute force for every secondary ray can be written in pure PyTorch, but by the estimate in the survey notes, 38% of 200×200 pixels (about 15 thousand rays) and 20 thousand Gaussians make about 300 million pairs, and a single intermediate tensor uses about 1.2 GB, so training is slow even when split into chunks. As for the order of experiments, it is best to first run the convex-shape control (a sphere or a vase) that the experiment write-up lists next, and measure, on both training and test views, how far the shared environment beats per-point SH under conditions without interreflection or fine bumps. As the 21.8 dB difference between ball and toaster in Ref-NeRF shows, the more convex and smooth the object, the better sharing by reflection direction works ([Ref-NeRF](https://arxiv.org/html/2112.03907v1)).

## Conclusion

The literature of the past few years can be read as a history of moving "where to store" view-dependent information: from the points, to a shared table over directions, and then to the scene itself. This repo's four failure modes correspond one-to-one to the four fallbacks that the methods have added over time. For failure mode (1) it is the shared environment, for (2) normal regularisation and parallax, for (3) visibility and tracing, and for (4) roughness and normal textures. The gold Erato statue hits all four at once, so neither any single line nor a benchmark average can predict how the statue behaves, and high existing numbers do not guarantee performance under the conditions of a statue, a near-mirror surface and nearby lights.

At the same time, this repo's setup is itself a tool that fills blind spots in the literature. Papers on glossy 3DGS do not report the gap between training and test views, have measured interreflection only as a small effect of 0.2–0.6 dB, and have no dataset that uses the normal distribution within the pixel as a metric. The combination of synthetic data that has ground-truth normals, visibility and a diffuse control, with a PyTorch implementation in which oracles can be removed one at a time, is a path chosen because of the M4000 constraint. It also makes possible a comparison that existing research has not made: decomposing the causes of the failures and attributing them. In particular, the attempt to put the normal distribution within the pixel into Gaussian deferred shading as an effective roughness is a gap in the literature, yet it is of a size that can be tested at the scale of 3dgs-toy.

## Addendum (2026-11-18): results of the oracle version of the third step

The idea of the third step was first checked with a point-by-point fit that does not use 3DGS ([Follow-up 3 of this repo's experiment](../README.md)). On top of the shared lighting, a blur over the reflection direction was added per point, and the blur width was set in 2 ways and compared.

- Convert it from the normal variation within the pixel measured on the true mesh (no parameters)
- Optimise it per point (in place of the learned roughness of existing methods)

On this statue, the two were almost uncorrelated (rank correlation 0.14; 0.17 even after correcting for the reliability of the optimised values). On synthetic data blurred exactly according to the conversion, the same procedure recovers up to 0.89, so the procedure is not underpowered. The median of the optimised blur was 6°, half of the conversion at a width of 1 pixel (12°). Where the converted blur was 6° or less, the result improved by 0.2 dB; where it was larger, the result got worse.

So the "gap" remains, but on this statue, where interreflection, nearby lights and near-cylindrical folds are all involved, putting the conversion in as is did not help (which of these is the cause has not been separated). Checking the conversion itself under conditions of a convex object, distant lighting and controlled bumps comes first.
